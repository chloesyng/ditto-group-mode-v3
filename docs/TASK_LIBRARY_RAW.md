Pre-date

Anonymous booklet
They fall for someone's mind before they see them in a room.

What happens: Each person answers 5 relationship dilemma questions in writing — who pays on a first date, how long until you'd introduce someone to your family, what you'd do if you read your partner's texts by accident. Booklets are distributed anonymously across gender lines. First pick is first served. Pairing is set by which booklet someone chooses.
Why it works: People project their ideal partner onto an anonymous voice. By the time the reveal happens, they've already decided they like the writer. The reveal becomes a payoff, not a first impression.
Touch level: None — chemistry is entirely anticipatory.

tell codex
Generate 5 dilemma questions calibrated to each user's stated relationship values. Build a booklet object per user. Distribute by gender/preference pairing. Output first pairing assignment.


Memory object blind pick
They choose a partner through emotional taste, not appearance.

What happens: One side of the group submits a photo of a meaningful place or object before the date. The other side picks a photo without knowing who submitted it. That photo determines the first pairing of the date.
Why it works: The pick feels fated. Everyone else in the group notices that pair was "chosen" — which makes both people more interesting to the others.
Touch level: None — but the chosen pair arrives already carrying a shared secret.
tell codex
Prompt each user to submit one photo with a 10-word caption max. Present photos to the other group stripped of names. Store assignment. Deliver "you picked each other" message at date start.


Early

The postcard pick
Public declaration disguised as a casual note.

Mechanic: On arrival, each person is asked to write a short anonymous note to whoever gave them the strongest first impression, then sends it to Ditto. Ditto send them all out to everyone without revealing who wrote which. The group guesses the author.
Why it works: Forces immediate preference formation. The guessing game exposes who's paying attention to whom — and who's flustered when their name comes up.
Touch level: None. But eye contact is unavoidable.
Tell Codex: Fire within the first 10 minutes. Store notes. Anonymise, and release to everyone. Ask the group to guess the author. 

The MBTI read
'I already knew you were an INTJ.' 'How?' 'The way you arranged the spoons.'
House of Feelings
Mechanic: Each person's MBTI (from their application) is known to ditto but not to the group. Ditto releases one letter at a time — just one dimension, not the full type — and the group guesses who it belongs to. After each guess round, the person confirms and explains one real thing about themselves that fits.
Why it works: Personality typing creates an instant framework for reading people. The guessing game forces everyone to form and express impressions. The self-explanation creates voluntary disclosure under cover of the game.
Touch level: None. But 'I knew it was you' moments create social bonding.
Tell Codex: Use the user's stated personality type or infer from their application answers. Release one dimension per round (I/E, N/S, T/F, J/P). Run 4 rounds.

Mid

Earn your paradise
Physical effort becomes a love language.
Single's Inferno
Mechanic: One gender competes in a physical or skill-based challenge. The top finisher earns a 'paradise ticket' — the right to invite one person from the other group for a private mini-experience (walk, dessert, view). The invitation is made publicly. Everyone watches.
Why it works: Status + visible choice + the experience of being (or not being) chosen. The person not picked now has something to prove. The person picked has to manage being desired in front of others.
Touch level: High incidental during competition. Chosen touch in the mini-date.
Tell Codex: Adapt the challenge to the venue. Timed cooking task, balance game, taste-test accuracy. Generate specific rules and a tiebreaker. Winner sends their pick via private ditto message. Announce publicly.

Dodgeball/physical team game
The rule is: stay linked to your partner. If you separate, you're out.


What happens: Pairs compete physically. One person can only attack; the other shields and must stay physically linked to their partner at all times. Eliminated pairs sit out. Winning pair earns pick power for the next romantic decision.
Why it works: Forced physical contact under adrenaline = peak misattribution of arousal. Being protected by someone registers in the body as care even when it's just a game.
Touch level: High cooperative — constant bodily contact required by the rules.
tell codex
Adapt to the date space. Small space = passing/balance game. Outdoor = running or target game. Indoor = reaction game. Generate exact rules, elimination conditions, and what the winner earns.




The 10-minute window
10 minutes alone with the person who already knows you. Then you have to come back.
EXchange
Mechanic: Each person gets one 10-minute private window they can use at any point in the date to pull one other person aside for a private conversation. No rules on what gets said. When they return, the group can see who pulled who.
Why it works: The act of being chosen for a private window is highly visible. What gets said is private. The combination of visible selection + private content creates maximum intrigue in the room.
Touch level: Chosen — the selection itself is the first touch.
Tell Codex: Issue the window at the start of mid-arc. Track usage. If a window is used, send both people into a private space with a single prompt: 'you have 10 minutes. use them.' No script. Log timing and pairing for use in late-arc tasks. This task is only applicable for long dates over 2 hours. 


Heart rate challenge
Your body already decided. Now everyone gets to find out.
Love Island
Mechanic: Each person wears a heart rate monitor (or self-reports on a 1–10 scale). They each take a turn doing something to get the group's attention — a short performance, a compliment, a physical challenge. After all turns, ditto reveals whose heart rate spiked the most for each person. It's not always who they're coupled with.
Why it works: The body doesn't lie. When the spike goes to someone unexpected, the person's current partner notices. The reveal creates immediate renegotiation of existing dynamics.
Touch level: High — performances require proximity, attention, and often physical demonstration.
Tell Codex: Adapt to a self-report version: after each person's turn, everyone privately rates their own reaction 1–10. Collect and reveal the highest spike per person at the end. Announce the full results table at once. This task is only applicable when the group has a device that can detect heart rate, such as, apple watches, airpods, etc. This should be confirmed pre-date. 


The fear pong table
Land the ball, issue a dare. Miss, take a drink. All of it is information.
House of Feelings
Mechanic: Two pairs face off in a pong-style game. Landing the ball earns the right to issue a personal challenge or question to one person on the other side. Questions/dares are pre-loaded in tiers by ditto based on user profiles. The other person can decline — but declining is noted publicly.
Why it works: The game provides cover for escalating intimacy. Physical competition + emotional risk + the option to decline creates a three-layer dynamic that reveals character at every turn.
Touch level: Incidental to cooperative — high fives, close proximity at the table, physical reactions to dares.
Tell Codex: Pre-generate 12 questions/dares per pair based on profiles. Tier 1 (first 4): light, funny. Tier 2 (middle 4): personal but safe. Tier 3 (last 4): present-moment, about people in the room. This task is only applicable when beer pong is available at the date location. 

The cooking class pair
Sparks fly when hands are occupied.
Nineteen to Twenty
Mechanic: Pairs are assigned to cook one dish together. The constraint: one person can only handle prep, the other can only handle heat/cooking. They have to negotiate every step. The dish is judged by the other pair — not on taste, but on how well they appeared to work together.
Why it works: Shared physical task with a constraint forces communication, closeness, and negotiation under mild pressure. Being judged on how you worked together (not the output) means people are watching each other, not the food.
Touch level: Cooperative — hands in proximity, passing ingredients, adjusting each other's work.
Tell Codex: Assign prep/heat roles randomly. Set a 12-minute timer. The other pair judges on one dimension only: 'did they work like they knew each other?' Announce the result publicly. The winning pair earns a 5-minute head start on the next activity. This task is only applicable when the date provides a kitchen and cooking opportunities, such as during a cooking workshop, or barbeque date, etc. 


Late
The kissing booth (adapted)
Blindfolded. A kiss from someone in the room. You have to guess who.
Love Island
Mechanic: One person is blindfolded. Others take turns giving them a romantic touch — tapping a shoulder, holding a hand for 3 seconds, brushing a wrist, hugging. The blindfolded person guesses who touched them after each one. Results revealed at the end.
Why it works: Removes visual identity from physical sensation. People are often surprised by whose touch they recognised — and even more surprised by whose they didn't. That information is intimate.
Touch level: Chosen — every interaction involves intentional physical contact.
Tell Codex: Avoid kisses. Rotate who is blindfolded. Log each guess and accuracy. Reveal: 'you correctly identified X but mistook Y for Z.'



The recoupling ceremony
Public choice. Someone might be left standing alone.
Love Island
Mechanic: One gender stands. The other chooses, one by one, who they want to pair with for the final activity or meal. Each choice is announced aloud. The last person to be chosen (or unchosen) is given a private ditto message with a consolation task.
Why it works: Public selection is the most high-stakes social moment in any dating format. Being chosen feels good. Being the last to be chosen is information. Choosing someone else's partner creates visible tension.
Touch level: Chosen — the act of walking toward someone and pairing up is inherently physical.
Tell Codex: Run after mid-arc. Randomise speaking order. Each person announces their pick aloud. If anyone is unchosen, send them a private ditto message: 'you have a free pass — you can interrupt one existing pair before the final activity.'

The nightly signal
One anonymous text. One person in the room. Every night.
Heart Signal
Mechanic: At the end of the date, each person sends one anonymous message to one other person via ditto. No names. The recipient receives it but doesn't know who sent it. They can reply once. The conversation is tracked. On the final round, ditto reveals all signal pairs.
Why it works: The anonymous message removes the pressure of direct confession. People say things in text they wouldn't say in person. The final reveal of who signalled whom is usually the most emotionally charged moment of the show.
Touch level: None. But the accumulation of signals determines who people move toward physically later.
Tell Codex: Run one signal round per date arc phase (early, mid, late). Collect anonymously. Allow one anonymous reply. Final reveal: show each person's full signal thread and who it was with. Run this as the closing mechanic.



Multi-phase
The couples photo shoot
'The hottest couple on earth.' Everyone votes. Your current partner is watching.
Single's Inferno
Mechanic: Pairs are assigned (or self-selected if pairs are already established). Each pair does a brief couples photo shoot at a location on the date. The group votes on which pair looked most like a real couple. Winners earn first say in the next pairing decision.
Why it works: The task creates physical closeness under the cover of performance. The vote forces everyone to publicly acknowledge which pair had the most natural chemistry — and that information is now in the room.
Touch level: Cooperative to chosen — the photo requires physical closeness and natural poses.
Tell Codex: Prompt each pair: 'you have 5 minutes and one location. make it look real.' Collect photos or descriptions. Anonymous vote via ditto. Announce winning pair and their new privilege.

Snog, marry, pie (adapted)
Three choices. Everyone's watching where each one lands.
Love Island
Mechanic: Each person privately tells ditto three choices: who they'd kiss, who they'd couple up with for a week, and who they'd pie (a playful rejection). Results are revealed one person at a time, out loud, in front of the group. The person being 'pied' reacts live.
Why it works: Public declaration of hierarchy. The comedy of the pie softens the sting of rejection but the information is real. The kiss choice reveals what people actually feel regardless of who they're currently paired with.
Touch level: Light incidental — the reveal moment usually produces an instinctive physical reaction (leaning away, laughing into someone's shoulder).
Tell Codex: Collect privately via ditto. Reveal sequentially. Pause after each person's reveal to let the room react before moving to the next. Do not rush this. The silence is part of the mechanic.

Truth or Dare (escalating)
The dare isn't the point. The choice is.

What happens: Cards drawn from Ditto. Each card is either a truth question or a dare. Dares range from whispering something to the person you're most curious about, to performing a 30-second slow dance with the person of their choice, to trading seats with their partner. Truths escalate from preferences to observations about the people in the room.
Why it works: The structure lets people do things they want to do anyway, covered by the card. The dare is permission. Watching who does what to whom is the whole show.
Touch level: Chosen — the person selects who they do the dare with.
tell codex
Generate truth and dare cards in tiers. Tier 1: safe, funny. Tier 2: mildly revealing. Tier 3: physically or emotionally proximate. Calibrate tier 3 to the group's relationship goals. Include at least 2 dares that require choosing a specific person.


Love Jenga
A game with a secret. Every block has a question on it.
EXchange
Mechanic: Pairs play Jenga where each block pulled reveals a question written on it. Questions escalate in intimacy across the game — early blocks are light, later blocks get personal. Knocking the tower = you answer the question on the last block you pulled.
Why it works: Physical tension from the game transfers to emotional tension from the questions. The tower falling is a release that makes the question feel earned rather than intrusive.
Touch level: Incidental to cooperative — leaning over the tower, steadying each other's hands.
Tell Codex: Generate 3 tiers of questions per user pair based on their profiles. Tier 1: opinions and preferences. Tier 2: past relationship patterns. Tier 3: observations about someone in the room right now. Never use trauma or family history. This task is only applicable for dates that are indoor/sedentary. 
The time room check-in
How much of your attention is on them right now? Give it a number.
EXchange
Mechanic: At two points mid-date, ditto sends each person a private check-in: 'on a scale of 0–100, how much of your attention is on [person X] right now?' The person they're asked about is their current pairing partner. Ditto tracks the change between check-ins.
Why it works: Quantifying attention forces people to be honest with themselves. The change between check-in 1 and check-in 2 reveals whether the pairing is building or fading. Ditto uses this data — but never shares the numbers directly.
Touch level: None. Internal only.
Tell Codex: Fire check-in 1 at 30% through the date. Check-in 2 at 65%. Calculate delta per pair. Use delta to inform final pairing decisions. If attention dropped >30 points for both people in a pair, they are candidates for the recoupling task.

Lie detector round
True or false. Everyone already thinks they know the answer.
House of Feelings
Mechanic: Each person receives 5 yes/no questions from ditto, calibrated to their profile and the events of the night. They answer aloud. The group votes on whether they believe the answer. Ditto reveals if the majority was right. No actual lie detector — the mechanic is social pressure.
Why it works: Social truth-telling. The questions are crafted to be about the night — 'did you feel something during the cooking task?' 'did you already know who you wanted to pair with before ditto assigned you?' The group's read of each other is usually more revealing than the answer.
Touch level: None. But emotional exposure usually produces physical closeness with the person they trust.
Tell Codex: Generate 5 questions per person based on their stated preferences, the night's events, and any signals ditto has logged. Questions must be answerable yes or no. Avoid anything that could cause genuine embarrassment.
